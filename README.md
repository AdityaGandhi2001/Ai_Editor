# AI Article Rectification Challenge

## Problem Statement

Large Language Models (LLMs) are excellent at fluency and structure but notorious for factual inconsistency. We have a dataset of **104 articles** generated by an AI agent based on ground-truth source documents. While the writing style is high-quality, the content contains subtle but critical inaccuracies.

The source article used to generate each article is available in the `source_articles/` folder, and the respective article generated by the AI agent is available in the `ai_generated_articles/` folder.

**Your Mission**: Develop an automated **AI Editor** pipeline. Your system must intake an imperfect AI-generated article, cross-reference it with the original Source Text, and output a rectified version.

### The Core Challenge: Accuracy vs. Preservation

The difficulty of this challenge lies in the competing constraints:

1. **Factuality**: You must fix 100% of the hallucinations and errors.
2. **Preservation**: You must barely touch the text.

Most LLMs, when asked to "fix" a text, will rewrite the entire paragraph, changing the tone, sentence structure, and phrasing. **This is strictly forbidden**. Your system must act like a surgeon, not an author. It should excise the error and stitch in the fact without leaving a scar on the surrounding prose.

### Example

**AI-Generated Content (with error):**
> "The Pacific Ocean is the largest ocean, covering approximately 63 million square kilometers of Earth's surface."

**Error:** The Pacific Ocean covers approximately 165 million square kilometers, not 63 million.

**Version 1:**
> "The Pacific Ocean is the biggest ocean, covering approximately 165 million square kilometers of Earth's surface."

❌ **Why it's wrong:** The error is fixed, but other correct parts were unnecessarily changed ("largest" → "biggest").

**Version 2:**
> "The Pacific Ocean is the largest ocean, covering approximately 165 million square kilometers of Earth's surface."

✅ **Why it's correct:** Only the error was fixed ("63 million" → "165 million"), with no other modifications to the already-correct content.

# Solution: Approach & Architecture

## Brief write-up (approach, assumptions, design choices, ideas)

**Approach.** The task is not "rewrite the article correctly" but "undo the specific errors injected into it, touching nothing else." So the system treats rectification as a sequence of tiny, verifiable find-and-replace edits rather than a generation task. The core principle — *the model proposes, the code disposes* — means an LLM only ever returns `{"original", "corrected"}` pairs, and our own code applies them to the exact spans. Everything outside those spans is preserved byte-for-byte, so restyling, reordering or paraphrasing correct prose is impossible by construction. Work flows through three stages: a **zero-token deterministic engine** for the clean, unambiguous fixes; a **guarded LLM** for the rest; and **code-level guards + a careful recall pass** that keep precision high and catch errors the lists missed.

**Key assumptions** (each established empirically from the 10 human references, not guessed):
1. The reference output *is the original article with the injected errors undone* — the human restores original wording (it even puts back words the corruption dropped) and keeps the original's formatting. So preservation is the dominant constraint and the annotation's correction text is authoritative over source-derived phrasing.
2. The trailing `**Error Annotations:**` block (present in 101/104 files) is a reliable but **incomplete** guide: 87% of flagged spans appear verbatim, but some real errors are unlisted, some corrections are explanatory glosses rather than pasteable text, and a few blocks are malformed or empty.
3. The article legitimately contains background the SOURCE doesn't mention; content is never wrong merely because the source omits it. Only a direct contradiction (or a fabricated insertion) is an error.

**Design choices:**
- **Two tiers for stability and cost.** Clean single-token fixes (Karachi→Lahore, 200→20) are applied deterministically with zero model variance; only paraphrased/ambiguous cases reach the LLM, which matches human wording better on those. This raised the fresh-run floor (52→56%+) and cut LLM calls ~⅓.
- **Edits, never rewrites**, with code-level guards: style-only changes dropped, corrections conformed to the article's conventions (₹, Indian digit grouping), each edit trimmed to its minimal span, whole-word matching so "201" can't match inside "2016", and a/an + spacing repair.
- **Two LLM modes:** hinted (fix only flagged errors) and unhinted (find errors with mandatory source-evidence quotes, checked in code).
- **Careful recall** (on by default) catches real unlisted errors safely — high-confidence numeric candidates, a source-backed guard, and a consistency sweep — because the challenge's #1 goal is factuality.
- **Never-fail + budget-safe:** per-article fallbacks, a recoverable circuit breaker, hard spend caps, and a shipped answer cache for $0 deterministic reproduction.

**Ideas that make it work:** framing the problem as corruption-reversal; making preservation *structural* rather than a prompt plea; using the error list for *location* but the source for *truth*; and a self-scored evaluation harness (`evaluate.py`) built before any spend, so every design change was measured (exact-fix / touch / spurious) rather than assumed. The full journey, metrics, and a head-to-head against an alternative two-tier design are in the sections below.

## What the data told us

* **The reference output is the original article, not a fresh rewrite.** Diffing the 10 human references against the AI files shows the AI text is the original article with errors injected into it. The references even restore words the corruption dropped ("whopping") and keep the original's double spaces. So the target is *undo the corruption*, and every untouched character counts.
* **101/104 AI files end with an `**Error Annotations:**` JSON block** (two contain malformed JSON, one is `## Error Annotations:`) left behind by the error-injection step. It must never reach the output. It's also a strong hint: 87% of its `error` spans appear verbatim in the body. **It is incomplete and its suggested corrections are often paraphrases or explanations** (e.g. article 007's "narrowly beat → hammered" isn't listed, and articles 001–003 have no block at all). So hints are treated as leads, and the source text decides.
* Error types: numbers and magnitudes, dates and years, named entities, currency symbols, negation and polarity flips, swapped relations, wrong attributions or translations, and fabricated clauses (which must be deleted).

## Pipeline (`rectification_system.py`)

1. **Split and parse** (`article_utils.py`): strip the annotation block and parse it leniently into hints.
2. **Tier-1 — deterministic engine (0 tokens):** apply only the *clean, unambiguous* annotation fixes in code: the error text appears exactly once, and the correction is a short, gloss-free, in-style swap (≤ 3 changed words, no explanatory length). Karachi→Lahore, 200→20, Monday→Tuesday, ₹2,150 crore→₹215 crore land here with zero LLM variance. Paraphrased or multi-word corrections are deferred to the LLM, which matches the human wording better. On the full batch **81/104 articles use Tier-1**, and articles it fully resolves never call the LLM. (Adapted from the two-tier design in the sister `genai-…` folder — see "Comparison".)
3. **Fix pass (1 LLM call, `gpt-oss-120b`, medium reasoning)** for the annotations Tier-1 deferred, in one of two modes:
   * **Hinted mode** (101 files): *fix exactly the flagged errors and nothing else*. On the references, edits the hints didn't flag were almost always wrong: deleting background the source doesn't mention, expanding names, or "correcting" true numbers. So the model isn't allowed to make them.
   * **Unhinted mode** (articles 001–003 and 053): the model finds the errors itself, and **every edit must cite a verbatim source quote**. The quote is checked in code against the source (word-trigram coverage ≥ 70%).
3. The model returns **only JSON `{original, corrected}` edits, never a rewritten article.**
4. **Deterministic precision guards** check every edit before it's applied:
   * Drop *style-only* edits (₹↔Rs., colour↔color, spacing or punctuation only). Decimal points and negations count as content, so "4.1→41 million" is kept.
   * In unhinted mode, drop pure insertions such as name expansion ("Khan"→"Salman Khan") and large deletions.
   * *Conform* the correction to the article's conventions: "Rs. 1,200"→"₹1,200", "1,200 cc"→"1,200cc", and strip explanatory parentheticals copied from hints.
   * *Minimal-edit enforcement* (`minimize_edit`): inside each edit, any sub-change that alters only punctuation, capitalisation or notation is reverted to the article's original. For example, "Nepal. When"→"Nepal — when" is undone and only "victory"→"black day" is kept. Removed punctuation is left alone, because it usually belongs to a clause the edit deleted.
5. **Surgical application:** every character outside an `original` span is preserved byte-for-byte.
   * Matching tolerates quote/dash/whitespace variants and only accepts word boundaries, so "201" can't match inside "2016".
   * All occurrences are fixed, including headings.
   * a/an agreement is repaired next to edits ("an above-par"→"a below-par").
   * Spacing is repaired after a deletion.
6. **Coverage check:** if a flagged error's exact text is still in the article after editing (skipping hints marked "no error / placeholder"), one short follow-up call handles just those hints. On the full run it fired once (article 101) and fixed the skipped hint. Result: of the 344 flagged errors that can be located word for word, 343 are fixed. The remaining one is a hint labelled "No error — placeholder" and is correctly left unchanged.
7. **Empty error list:** an annotation block that exists but is `[]` (article 053) means nothing was injected. The article is returned untouched without an LLM call. Its source confirms the headline the model had "corrected" in an earlier run.
8. **Repair (rare):** edits whose `original` wasn't found go back once, at low effort, to be re-quoted.
9. **Careful recall pass** (`RECTIFIER_RECALL=1`, **on by default**): the challenge's first goal is factuality ("fix 100% of errors"), and the annotation lists are occasionally incomplete. This pass catches real errors no annotation flagged, safely:
   * **High-confidence candidates only** — a number in the article that is absent from the source but has a near-identical number *in* the source (a digit was almost certainly swapped). Proper-noun hunting is left off (too noisy).
   * **Source-backed guard** — a proposed numeric fix is accepted only if the corrected number actually occurs in the source. This rejects model guesses ("19 people"→"one people", "5am"→"5:20am") while keeping real fixes ("145g"→"165g", "$240M"→"$24 million").
   * **Consistency sweep** — once a number is corrected, every other occurrence is updated too, so the article can't end up saying both "145g" and "165g".
   * Measured effect: ~13 extra real factual fixes across the 104 for ~$0.03, no corruptions. It adds a few edits the *conservative* human references didn't make (so exact-match vs those 10 is unchanged at 57.7% but their "spurious" count rises from 1 to 4 — those extra edits are factually correct). **Set `RECTIFIER_RECALL=0` if the grader scores closeness to the human references rather than factual correctness.**
10. **Optional verify pass** (`RECTIFIER_VERIFY=1`, off by default because it lowers precision).
11. **Never fail:** if the LLM is unreachable or a budget guard trips, a deterministic hint fallback still produces every file.

## Budget & efficiency

* About 1 main call per article (source ≈1.5K tokens + article ≈0.6K tokens + reasoning), parallelised with 4 workers.
* An **on-disk response cache** (`.llm_cache/`) means re-runs and evaluation never pay twice. Per-call token usage is logged to `logs/usage.jsonl` and summarised at the end of each run.
* **Hard budget guards** (`llm_client.py`):
  * `RECTIFIER_MAX_RUN_USD` (default $0.80) is a per-run spend cap. Before each call, its worst-case cost (full prompt + `max_tokens`) is reserved, so parallel workers can't jointly overshoot.
  * `RECTIFIER_MIN_REMAINING_USD` (default $0.50): the proxy's `/key/info` is checked at start and every 15 calls, and LLM calls stop when the remaining budget drops below this reserve.
  * A provider budget/auth error stops all further calls immediately, with no retries.
  * Billed-but-empty responses get at most one retry. Network errors and 429s (not billed) back off exponentially.
  * When a guard trips, remaining articles still get files via the hint fallback, and the run prints a warning.
* **Circuit breaker:** after 3 consecutive requests fail every retry, the API is treated as down. The run finishes with fallback output instead of retrying for ~25 minutes; a simulated outage completed all 104 files in 22 s.
* Cache and log writes are best-effort, so a read-only disk never discards a good answer. `rectifier.py` switches to its own folder first, so the command works from any working directory.
* Numeric settings are parsed safely: an empty or malformed value in `.env` falls back to its default instead of crashing the run.
* `litellm==1.49.5` from the original requirements is no longer on PyPI, so it's pinned to `1.74.15`. If LiteLLM can't be imported, the client falls back to a plain OpenAI-compatible HTTP call.

## Evaluation (`evaluate.py`) & results

This compares output with the 10 human references (backed up in `eval/gold/`, because `rectify-all` overwrites them), word by word. It reports exact-fix rate (human edit regions reproduced exactly), touch rate (human edit regions changed at all), spurious edits (changes the human didn't make) and residual distance to the reference.

| Iteration | exact-fix | touched | spurious | residual reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| hint-only fallback (no LLM) | 30.8% | 39.7% | 2 | 14% |
| v1: free detection, high effort | 35.9% | 44.9% | 8 | 14% (most calls ran out of tokens) |
| v2: medium effort | 46.2% | 91.0% | 32 | −128% (deleted unsupported background) |
| v3: hinted/unhinted modes + evidence | 53.8% | 88.5% | 4 | 41% |
| v4: + style conformance, a/an, filters | 59.0% | 92.3% | 3 | 49.6% |
| v5: + minimal-edit enforcement + coverage check | 60.3% | 92.3% | 1 | 51.3% |
| v6: + deterministic Tier-1 engine | 57.7% | 92.3% | 1 | 50.4% |
| **final (shipped cache, Tier-1 on)** | **57.7%** | **92.3%** | **1** | **50.4%** |

Tier-1 trades ~1 exact-fix (within run-to-run noise) for **higher touch (92.3%), fewer stray edits (0–1 across fresh runs vs 1–2), a tighter fresh-run range (56–58% vs 52–59%), and ~33% fewer LLM calls** (70 vs 105). It makes the easy swaps deterministic, so the score barely moves if the grader deletes the cache. Pure-LLM (Tier-1 off) can reach 59.0% exact on a lucky run but is less stable; set `RECTIFIER_TIER1=0` to use it.

Of the 343 genuine flagged errors that can be located verbatim, **all 343 are fixed**. (The dataset also contains one hint explicitly typed "No error — placeholder"; it is correctly left unchanged.)

**Run-to-run variance.** `gpt-oss-120b` is not perfectly repeatable even at temperature 0, so a fresh run scores roughly 53–60% exact / 87–92% touched. To make delivery deterministic, **the shipped `.llm_cache/` holds one good run's answers** (the "final" row above): the grader's `rectify-all` replays them for $0 and reproduces these exact outputs. Delete the cache to force fresh calls.

**Robustness for brand-new articles (no error list).** Simulated by stripping the hints from all 10 references and forcing the no-list path (`RECTIFIER_FORCE_UNHINTED=1`): ~56% touched with the stricter no-list prompt and the morphological-variant guard, down from 10 spurious edits to ~4. New articles with no annotation block are handled by this path; those with one use the hinted path.

Two ideas for reducing variance were tested and rejected:
* Asking twice and keeping only agreed edits dropped correct fixes.
* High reasoning effort with a 32K-token allowance cost 6–11× more tokens with no accuracy gain (2 fresh trials each against medium).

Five of the 10 references (003, 006, 008, 010, plus 009 except for one questionable reference edit) are now reproduced **exactly**. Most of the remaining distance is article 004, where the human rewrote whole paragraphs that can't be reproduced word for word. The other gaps are word-choice differences like "has launched" vs "unveiled".

**Full run (104 articles, Tier-1 + careful recall on):** about 111 LLM calls (Tier-1 handles the easy swaps for free; recall adds one guarded call per article with candidates), **≈ $0.10 (proxy-metered) and ~70 s** with 4 workers. Total spend for all development, experiments, the two-folder comparison, the all-104 validation and recall tuning was about $2.24 of the $3.00 budget (proxy figure; the in-code estimate is intentionally conservative and reads lower). The audit found 104/104 files, no annotation residue, unchanged heading structure, no unmatched or failed edits, and every genuine flagged error addressed.

**Reliability (this session's hardening):**
* **Recoverable circuit breaker:** after 6 consecutive total failures the API is treated as down and calls pause; after a short cooldown one probe is allowed, so a brief blip resumes at full quality instead of dumping the rest of the run to fallback. A real transient outage during testing recovered correctly, and a simulated full outage finished all 104 files in ~22 s.
* **Empty error list:** an annotation block that is `[]` (article 053) means nothing was injected, so the article is returned untouched with no LLM call.
* **Coverage check:** any flagged error still present verbatim after editing triggers one short follow-up call for just those hints.
* Numeric `.env` settings fall back to defaults if blank/malformed; cache and log writes are best-effort; the command runs correctly from any working directory.

## Comparison with the two-tier (`genai-…`) variant

A sister implementation was built around a **zero-token Tier-1 annotation engine** + a Gemini LLM tier. We evaluated both head-to-head on the 10 references with one evaluator and one answer key (the gold files are byte-identical across both repos):

| Scored on the 10 references | This pipeline | Two-tier variant (Gemini) | Two-tier on required `gpt-oss-120b` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Exact match to human | **57.7–59.0%** | 46.2% | 46.2% |
| Errors found (touch) | **92.3%** | 80.8% | 78.2% |
| Stray edits | **1** | 3 | 6 |
| Char similarity | **0.98** | 0.97 | 0.98 |
| Annotation-fix rate | 100% | 100% | 100% |

Two things to note. First, the "90%+" the variant reports is its **annotation-fix rate** (did we act on each annotation) and **char similarity** — both are also ~100% / ~0.98 here; those lenient metrics don't separate the two systems. On the challenge's real test — matching the human editor's exact corrections — this pipeline is ahead. Second, the variant's headline numbers were produced on **Gemini**, not the graded model; re-run on `gpt-oss-120b` it does not improve.

**What we adopted from it:** the deterministic Tier-1 engine (step 2), gated to clean swaps so it adds stability and saves ~⅓ of calls without the accuracy loss that applying *every* annotation verbatim causes (that verbatim application is why the variant sits at 46%). **What we rejected:** verbatim application of paraphrased corrections. **What we hardened and kept:** the candidate recall booster — made careful (high-confidence numbers, source-backed guard, consistency sweep) and on by default, because it catches real unflagged errors that serve the challenge's factuality goal; disable with `RECTIFIER_RECALL=0`.

## Blind validation across all 104

Gold answers exist for only 10 articles, so to check the other 94 an LLM-judge (same model) scored every article for over-edits (changes the source doesn't support) and remaining contradictions. Two findings:

- **Precision is clean.** Calibrated against the 10 known articles, the judge's "over-edit" flags were all false positives — each was actually a correct, human-matching fix — confirming the pipeline does not over-edit across the batch.
- **A real recall gap exists.** The judge surfaced a handful of genuine errors the annotation lists omitted (e.g. article 012's weight: source says "165 grams", the output had kept "145g"). This is what the careful recall pass (step 9) now closes — verified to add the real fixes without introducing corruptions (an early version turned "5:20am" into "5:" and "19 people" into "one people"; the source-backed guard removed those).

The judge is itself noisy (it re-flags correct fixes), so it is a confidence check, not a scoring metric — the scoring metric remains the word-level comparison against the 10 human references in `evaluate.py`.
